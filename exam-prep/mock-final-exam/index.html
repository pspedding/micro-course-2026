    🎯 Mock Final Exam — Microeconomics

    USYD ECON1001 style · 20 MCQ (40 marks) + 3 analytical questions (60 marks) · 2 hours

    Exam technique

    • For MCQ: eliminate wrong answers, then compare the remaining options carefully.
    • For calculations: write formula → substitute → solve. Show working.
    • For short answers: state the concept first, then apply it to the numbers in the question.

    Section A — Multiple Choice (20 questions)

    Question 1

    Section A · Price Controls

    A price ceiling set below the equilibrium price will usually create:

    • A) A surplus
    • B) A shortage
    • C) No effect on the market
    • D) Higher producer surplus
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) A shortage

    A binding price ceiling prevents the price from rising to the equilibrium level, so quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.

    Question 2

    Section A · Price Controls

    A price floor set above the equilibrium price will usually create:

    • A) A shortage
    • B) A surplus
    • C) No effect on the market
    • D) Lower consumer surplus only
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) A surplus

    A binding price floor keeps the price above equilibrium, so quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

    Question 3

    Section A · Taxes and Incidence

    A tax of $20 per unit is imposed on consumers. Demand is P = 60 − 4Q and supply is P = 6Q. How much more do consumers pay, and how much less do sellers receive?

    • A) Consumers pay $12 more; sellers receive $8 less
    • B) Consumers pay $8 more; sellers receive $12 less
    • C) Consumers pay $20 more; sellers receive $0 less
    • D) Consumers pay $4 more; sellers receive $16 less
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Consumers pay $8 more; sellers receive $12 less

    Pre-tax equilibrium: 60 − 4Q = 6Q 60 = 10Q Q = 6 P = 36

    With a $20 tax on consumers: Pc = Ps + 20 Demand: Pc = 60 − 4Q Supply: Ps = 6Q

    So: 60 − 4Q = 6Q + 20 40 = 10Q Q = 4

    Ps = 6(4) = 24 and Pc = 24 + 20 = 44.

    Consumers pay 44 − 36 = \(8 more**. Sellers receive 24 − 36 = **\)12 less.

    Question 4

    Section A · Tax Incidence

    If supply is perfectly elastic and a tax is imposed on sellers, then:

    • A) Sellers bear the full tax burden
    • B) Buyers bear the full tax burden
    • C) The burden is split equally
    • D) No deadweight loss is created
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Buyers bear the full tax burden

    A perfectly elastic supply curve means sellers will supply any amount at a given net price. A tax on sellers shifts the supply curve upward by the tax amount, so buyers pay the full increase in price. Because quantity falls, there is still deadweight loss.

    Question 5

    Section A · Taxes and Welfare

    Petrol demand is D = 280 − 4Q and supply is S = 10 + Q. A tax of $20 per unit is imposed on consumers. What are the post-tax quantity, government revenue, and deadweight loss?

    • A) Q = 54, revenue = $1080, DWL = $0
    • B) Q = 50, revenue = $1000, DWL = $40
    • C) Q = 50, revenue = $1080, DWL = $100
    • D) Q = 54, revenue = $1000, DWL = $40
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Q = 50, revenue = $1000, DWL = $40

    Pre-tax equilibrium: 280 − 4Q = 10 + Q 270 = 5Q Q* = 54, P* = 64

    With a consumer tax: Pc = Ps + 20 Demand: Pc = 280 − 4Q Supply: Ps = 10 + Q

    So: 280 − 4Q = 10 + Q + 20 250 = 5Q Q = 50

    Revenue = 20 × 50 = $1000

    DWL = 0.5 × 4 × 20 = $40

    Question 6

    Section A · Externalities

    A negative externality causes market output to be:

    • A) Too low relative to the social optimum
    • B) Too high relative to the social optimum
    • C) Exactly efficient
    • D) Zero in all cases
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Too high relative to the social optimum

    The market ignores part of the social cost, so price is too low and quantity too high. A Pigouvian tax equal to the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity can correct this.

    Question 7

    Section A · Public Goods

    Which combination best describes a public good?

    • A) Rival and excludable
    • B) Non-rival and excludable
    • C) Rival and non-excludable
    • D) Non-rival and non-excludable
    Show Answer

    Answer: D) Non-rival and non-excludable

    Public goods are non-rival because one person's consumption does not reduce availability for others, and non-excludable because it is difficult to stop non-payers from benefiting. This creates the free-rider problem.

    Question 8

    Section A · Common Resources

    The tragedy of the commons applies to a good that is:

    • A) Rival and non-excludable
    • B) Non-rival and excludable
    • C) Non-rival and non-excludable
    • D) Rival and excludable
    Show Answer

    Answer: A) Rival and non-excludable

    Common resources can be used by many people, but each use reduces what is left for others. Because exclusion is difficult, individuals overuse the resource.

    Question 9

    Section A · Public Goods

    Why do economists vertically sum marginal benefits for public goods?

    • A) Because each person consumes a separate unit
    • B) Because one unit is enjoyed simultaneously by everyone
    • C) Because demand curves are always straight lines
    • D) Because supply is perfectly inelastic
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Because one unit is enjoyed simultaneously by everyone

    For a public good, everyone consumes the same unit at the same time, so total marginal benefit is the sum of each person’s marginal willingness to pay at that quantity. That is vertical summation.

    Question 10

    Section A · Trade and Opportunity Cost

    Comparative advantage means:

    • A) A country can produce more of every good
    • B) A country has lower opportunity cost in producing a good
    • C) A country has the same absolute advantage in all goods
    • D) A country always exports manufactured goods
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) A country has lower opportunity cost in producing a good

    Comparative advantage is about relative efficiency, not absolute productivity. Gains from trade come from specialising according to opportunity cost.

    Question 11

    Section A · Gains from Trade

    Even if one country has an absolute advantage in all goods, trade can still be beneficial because:

    • A) Absolute advantage is all that matters
    • B) Comparative advantage still determines the gains from specialisation
    • C) Trade always makes both countries poorer
    • D) Tariffs remove all gains from trade
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Comparative advantage still determines the gains from specialisation

    Absolute advantage means producing more with the same resources, but comparative advantage determines which good a country should specialise in. Trade can benefit both countries if each specialises in the good with the lower opportunity cost.

    Question 12

    Section A · Public Goods

    Alex has MBA = 24 − G and Beatrice has MBB = 12 − 0.5G. What is the market-wide marginal social benefit?

    • A) MSB = 12 − 0.5G
    • B) MSB = 24 − G
    • C) MSB = 36 − 1.5G
    • D) MSB = 36 − 0.5G
    Show Answer

    Answer: C) MSB = 36 − 1.5G

    For a public good, we vertically sum the marginal benefits: MSB = (24 − G) + (12 − 0.5G) = 36 − 1.5G

    Question 13

    Section A · Coase Theorem

    The Coase theorem says that if property rights are clear and transaction costs are zero:

    • A) Markets always fail
    • B) Private bargaining leads to the efficient outcome
    • C) Government must set the correct tax
    • D) The polluter always wins
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Private bargaining leads to the efficient outcome

    Under those assumptions, parties can bargain to the socially efficient level of pollution regardless of who holds the property right. The initial assignment affects distribution, not efficiency.

    Question 14

    Section A · Externalities and Policy

    In the education externality problem, the private market chooses Q = 300 while the social optimum is Q = 400. What policy corrects the under-provision?

    • A) A tax of $200 per unit
    • B) A subsidy of $200 per unit
    • C) A price ceiling
    • D) A quota that reduces quantity below 300
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) A subsidy of $200 per unit

    Education has a positive externality. At the efficient quantity, the external benefit is MEB = 400/2 = $200, so a per-unit subsidy of $200 aligns private and social incentives.

    Question 15

    Section A · Surplus

    Consumer surplus is:

    • A) The area above demand and below price
    • B) The area under demand and above price
    • C) Total revenue minus fixed costs
    • D) The area under supply and above price
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) The area under demand and above price

    Consumer surplus measures willingness to pay above the actual price paid. It is the net benefit buyers receive from participating in the market.

    Question 16

    Section A · Taxes and Welfare

    Deadweight loss from a tax is best described as:

    • A) Revenue collected by the government
    • B) The loss of total surplus that is not transferred to the government
    • C) Producer revenue after tax
    • D) Consumer surplus only
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) The loss of total surplus that is not transferred to the government

    A tax reduces consumer and producer surplus, but part of that reduction becomes tax revenue. Deadweight loss is the remaining efficiency loss from reduced trade.

    Question 17

    Section A · Elasticity

    If |PED| > 1, demand is:

    • A) Inelastic
    • B) Unit elastic
    • C) Elastic
    • D) Perfectly inelastic
    Show Answer

    Answer: C) Elastic

    When demand is elastic, quantity responds proportionally more than price. The less elastic side of the market bears more of the tax burden.

    Question 18

    Section A · Perfect Competition

    In perfect competition, a firm’s profit-maximising rule is:

    • A) P = MR + MC
    • B) MR = 0
    • C) P = MC in the short run; P = min AC in long-run equilibrium
    • D) P always exceeds MC
    Show Answer

    Answer: C) P = MC in the short run; P = min AC in long-run equilibrium

    A competitive firm chooses output where P = MC as long as price covers AVC in the short run. In the long run, entry and exit push economic profit to zero, so price equals minimum average cost.

    Question 19

    Section A · Monopoly

    A monopolist differs from a perfectly competitive firm because it:

    • A) Sets price equal to marginal cost
    • B) Produces where MR = MC and typically charges P > MC
    • C) Always earns zero profit
    • D) Has no demand curve
    Show Answer

    Answer: B) Produces where MR = MC and typically charges P > MC

    The monopolist chooses output where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, then reads price from the demand curve. Because the demand curve slopes downward, price exceeds marginal cost and creates deadweight loss relative to competition.

    Question 20

    Section A · Game Theory

    A Nash equilibrium is a situation where:

    • A) Each player chooses a strategy that is a best response to the other players' strategies
    • B) Everyone chooses the same strategy
    • C) One player always wins
    • D) Cooperation is impossible
    Show Answer

    Answer: A) Each player chooses a strategy that is a best response to the other players' strategies

    At a Nash equilibrium, no player can improve their payoff by changing strategy unilaterally. That is the definition of mutual best response.

    Section B — Analytical Questions (60 marks)

    Question 21
    Negative externalities and policy
    20 marks

    (i) Draw and explain a market with a negative externality, showing MPC, MSC, the market equilibrium, the socially efficient quantity, and deadweight loss.
    (ii) Using the lead battery numbers, calculate the Pigouvian tax: demand P = 200 − 2Q, MPC = 20 + Q, MEC = Q.
    (iii) Why might Coase bargaining fail in environmental pollution cases?

    Show Answer

    (i) Diagram and explanation

    A negative production externality means the private market ignores part of the cost imposed on third parties.

    • MPC is the firm's private supply curve.
    • MSC = MPC + MEC lies above MPC.
    • The market equilibrium occurs where demand intersects MPC, so output is too high.
    • The socially efficient outcome is where demand intersects MSC, so output is lower.
    • The deadweight loss is the triangle between demand, MSC, and the market quantity between Q* and Q_opt.

    In words: the market overproduces because it only considers private cost, not social cost.

    (ii) Pigouvian tax

    Competitive equilibrium: 200 − 2Q = 20 + Q 180 = 3Q Q* = 60

    Price: P = 20 + 60 = 80*

    Social marginal cost: MSC = MPC + MEC = (20 + Q) + Q = 20 + 2Q

    Efficient output: 200 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q 180 = 4Q Q_opt = 45

    Pigouvian tax at the efficient quantity: MEC = 45 So the tax should be $45 per unit.

    (iii) Why Coase may fail

    Coase bargaining often fails because environmental harm usually affects many people, so transaction costs are high. It is hard to identify every affected party, measure damages precisely, and prevent free-riding. Information is also imperfect, and bargaining across many parties is costly. For pollution, these frictions make market bargaining much harder than the Coase theorem’s idealised assumptions.

    Question 22
    Public goods and common resources
    20 marks

    (i) Explain the free-rider problem with an example.
    (ii) For the lighthouse example, Alex has MBA = 24 − G and Beatrice has MBB = 12 − 0.5G, with MC = 30. Find the efficient quantity of G.
    (iii) How does the tragedy of the commons differ from the public goods problem?

    Show Answer

    (i) Free-rider problem

    A free rider is someone who enjoys the benefit of a public good without paying for it. Because public goods are non-excludable, individuals know they cannot easily be excluded, so they may withhold payment and hope others fund the good.

    Example: If a town finances a fireworks display, each resident may prefer others to pay while still enjoying the show. If everyone reasons this way, the private market underprovides the good.

    (ii) Lighthouse efficiency

    Vertical summation: MSB = (24 − G) + (12 − 0.5G) = 36 − 1.5G

    Efficient quantity: 36 − 1.5G = 30 1.5G = 6 G = 4

    So the efficient number of lighthouses is 4.

    (iii) Commons vs public goods

    The public goods problem is caused by non-excludability and non-rivalry, which leads to under-provision because of free-riding.

    The tragedy of the commons occurs with rival but non-excludable goods. The problem there is overuse, because each person’s use reduces availability for others but no one can be easily excluded.

    So public goods are typically under-provided, while common resources are typically over-exploited.

    Question 23
    Price floors and trade
    20 marks

    (i) In a labour market, demand is D = 400 − 2w and supply is S = 240 + 2w. Find the equilibrium wage and employment. Then suppose a price floor of $50 is imposed. Find employment, unemployment, and deadweight loss.
    (ii) Explain comparative advantage.
    (iii) Why do economists generally favour free trade even when it creates winners and losers domestically?

    Show Answer

    (i) Labour market calculation

    Equilibrium: 400 − 2w = 240 + 2w 160 = 4w w* = 40

    Employment at equilibrium: Q = 400 − 2(40) = 320*

    With a price floor of $50: Quantity demanded: Q_D = 400 − 2(50) = 300

    Quantity supplied: Q_S = 240 + 2(50) = 340

    Employment is limited by demand, so actual employment = 300.

    Unemployment = Q_S − Q_D = 340 − 300 = 40

    Deadweight loss: DWL = 0.5 × (50 − 40) × (340 − 300) = 0.5 × 10 × 40 = $200

    (ii) Comparative advantage

    Comparative advantage means producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer or country. It is about relative efficiency, not absolute productivity.

    (iii) Why economists favour free trade

    Economists generally favour free trade because it allows countries to specialise according to comparative advantage, raising total world output and welfare. Even if some domestic workers or firms lose, the total gains from trade are large enough that the winners can in principle compensate the losers and still be better off. Free trade also increases variety, lowers prices, and encourages more efficient resource allocation.
